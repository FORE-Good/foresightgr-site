## Background, Objectives and Methodology

*What the research set out to understand and how to read the evidence.*

### Background and Objectives

FORE Good developed a product concept aimed at supporting for-purpose organisations with government engagement work.

The product, a subscription platform, is currently conceptualised as a two-tier offering encompassing:

- A template library (Tier 1 and Tier 2)
- AI policy intelligence (Tier 1 and Tier 2)
- Advisory calls (Tier 2 only)

Market research was sought to test the product with senior leaders in the for-purpose sector, assessing product appeal, motivators and barriers to uptake, strengths and weaknesses, willingness to pay — ultimately, providing recommendations regarding the go-to-market strategy and any suggestions for improvement.

### Methodology

- **10 structured in-depth interviews** with senior leaders in the for-purpose sector across a range of organisation sizes, sectors, government engagement levels and internal capability.

- **Desktop research** into the sector to identify sectors and organisations which may potentially be a good fit for the product based on the primary research findings.

Interviews covered current exposure to and engagement with government, policy and advocacy work, detailed exploration of product appeal and perceptions as well as suggestions for improvement. Limited testing of product name was conducted (where appropriate given the tone of the discussion).

## Government Engagement Context

*Government engagement matters, but capacity is thin, ad hoc and often personally held by already-stretched leaders.*

### Government Engagement Generally Matters but It Is Not Always a Priority

- For many for-purpose organisations, government shapes the operating environment: funding, regulation, legitimacy, program access, public systems and policy settings.
- Many participants recognised government engagement as strategically important, however, often it was not a priority with operational aspects (i.e. service delivery) taking precedence in resource constrained organisations.
- Government engagement is often happening indirectly: through grants, tenders, stakeholder conversations and evidence-sharing. Given its linkage to service delivery, obtaining funding through grants was often the primary 'government engagement' element. For many participants, active policy and advocacy work did not immediately surface as being important or something where time and effort was invested.

> "We're just starting to tap into that space because now we're starting to see that there is really a lot [that the government] can actually deploy [in terms of] national strategy."

**The opportunity is to help organisations move from opportunistic or reactive contact to a more deliberate operating rhythm.**

### What Makes Government Engagement Difficult

| Pain point | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| **No dedicated owner** | Government relations work sits with CEOs, founders, board members, fundraising, comms or program staff. *"It's all of us. So we're a small team, but very multi-skilled."* |
| **Low government relations literacy** | Some know the issue deeply but not how government works, who to approach or what good looks like. *"I wouldn't even know where to start."* |
| **Reactive mode** | Organisations respond to issues, grants, tenders or inquiries rather than planning ahead. *"The way we respond to things is on the reactive as opposed to proactive."* |
| **Reputation/risk anxiety** | One participant was concerned about looking partisan or damaging stakeholder goodwill. *"We have to be neutral and bipartisan and focused on [who we serve], not a policy issue."* |
| **Lack of access to government contacts** | Some feel that they are well equipped internally but lack access to relevant government contacts. *"We know what to do… we just need to get in front of the right people."* |

### Market Has an Abundance of Content but Lacks Judgement

| Need | Question |
|---|---|
| **Relevance filtering** | What developments matter to this mission, jurisdiction and funding model? |
| **Interpretation** | What does this signal mean, how important is it and what does it unlock? |
| **Action framing** | What should we do next, who should we speak to and what should we prepare? |
| **Confidence** | Are we approaching this in the right way and avoiding obvious mistakes? |
| **Preparation** | Can we start from a good structure rather than a blank page? |
| **Prioritisation** | What should we ignore, watch, act on now, or escalate? |

> "It's more [about needing] a tick box check on are we doing the right thing?"

### Constraints That Don't Automatically Translate into a Potential Product Need

- **Budget is usually tight:** every subscription competes with service delivery, fundraising, technology, volunteer management and operational infrastructure.
- **Capability is uneven:** the same tool can be empowering for one organisation and overwhelming for another.
- **Time is a more important constraint than willingness:** even motivated leaders may not have capacity to act on intelligence or templates.
- **Needs are often episodic:** funding rounds, inquiries, elections, crises, grants, meetings and campaigns create bursts of need rather than even monthly demand.
- **Political risk matters:** some organisations need help engaging constructively without looking partisan or combative.

## Perceptions of, and Response to, the Product

*Overall response was mixed and conditional: compelling when it felt like guided action, weaker when perceived as a library, alert feed or a rigid subscription.*

### Appeal Was Mixed but There Is a Real Opportunity

> "It almost becomes an embedded thing that we're doing weekly around our mission and our strategy plan."

> "This assumes that you know how to do government engagement."

> "Stripping back bureaucracy to make it accessible to people. That's my first thought."

- Participants generally understood the underlying concept: helping organisations do government engagement themselves rather than outsourcing it completely.
- The strongest positive response came when the offer was imagined as an embedded operating element: a person owns it, intelligence comes in, templates support preparation and advisory calls shape action.
- The weakest responses came when the product was read as a self-serve resource hub: useful in theory, but not enough to overcome the capability and prioritisation gap.

**The central market test is not whether organisations would value "government engagement support" but whether the product makes action easier, safer and more worthwhile.**

### Some Participants Immediately Understood the Components While Others Saw a List of Features Without a Clear Outcome

#### Perceived Strengths

- Could provide discipline and structure for organisations currently "winging it".
- Can build internal confidence and capability rather than creating dependency.
- Fills the affordability gap between doing nothing and hiring a full-service government relations agency.
- Has clear value when tied to live policy, funding or stakeholder engagement goals.

> "It's that proactive piece that I would love to be ticking."

#### Perceived Weaknesses

- Risk of sounding like another subscription platform rather than a practical support system.
- Unclear what outcome a user gets from the product if they lack capability or ownership.
- A fixed monthly model may not match episodic need.
- Adviser quality, relevance and credibility are unclear and critical.
- Doesn't provide access to decision makers which is often a key gap.

> "A subscription platform doesn't empower you to do anything."

> "Do I get to speak to the minister and [have] the opportunity to present what we actually do on the ground?"

### Template Library

#### Perceived Strengths

- Valuable for teams that currently start from scratch or "wing it".
- Can save time and reduce anxiety around what a good document looks like.
- Useful as pre-work before advisory calls: drafts, checklists, issue briefs, stakeholder maps, meeting prep.
- Helps standardise practice and build organisational confidence.

> "The template library is something that we've built slowly. Is it best practice? We don't really know."

#### Perceived Weaknesses

- Less relevant for people who are more proficient.
- Not highly differentiated if it is just a static document library.
- Assumes the user knows which template to use, when, and how to adapt it.
- May be consumed once, reducing renewal value.
- Can feel generic unless tailored to jurisdiction, sector and scenario.

> "I could probably get ChatGPT to make me one that would be good enough in most cases."

#### Suggestions for Improvement

- Make it workflow-led, not document-led: "prepare for a ministerial meeting", "respond to an inquiry", "write a one-page budget ask", "turn evidence into a policy brief".
- Include examples of completed outputs, not just blank templates.
- Connect every template to a likely advisory question and a next action.
- Use the library as pre-work to increase the value and efficiency of advisory calls.
- Segment templates by maturity: beginner explanations, intermediate workflows and advanced checklists.

### AI Policy Intelligence

#### Perceived Strengths

- Can save time currently spent manually monitoring inquiries, grants, tenders and government announcements.
- Can help smaller organisations avoid missing policy windows.
- Useful if it points to relevance, deadline, risk, opportunity and recommended action.

> "How do we respond to what's happening through Parliament in real time? It's that proactive piece that I would love to be ticking."

#### Perceived Weaknesses

- Ethical or environmental concerns about AI were raised by at least one participant.
- May be compared with ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, newsletters or public websites unless clearly differentiated.
- Low value if it becomes generic alerts or unprioritised noise.
- High trust requirement: accuracy, sources and fact-checking matter.

> "How is it different [from] what's available LLMs?"

#### Suggestions for Improvement

- Make the benefit/outcome clear and tangible to differentiate from generic content that users can obtain themselves.
- Clearly link to other product components – where does it sit in the process, how does it help?
- Most suggestions/expectations centred around these needing to be tailored (sector, jurisdiction, portfolio) to be useful which could be considered in the longer term. This included users filling out a form during the subscription process describing their organisation and sector so that AI Policy Intelligence reports can be tailored and then specifically linked to the 'so what' for them (incl. recommendations on next steps and using other product components).

### Advisory Calls

#### Perceived Strengths

- Participants value judgement, not simply access to a person on a call.
- Advisory can translate intelligence into action, help prioritise, and reduce fear of getting it wrong.
- The right adviser can provide process navigation, context, sense-checking and practical next steps.

> "The 30 minute strategy sessions with someone who's been in the roles, I mean, that's the diamond at the end of this process, right?"

#### Perceived Weaknesses

- Value depends heavily on adviser quality, relevance and sector/jurisdiction expertise.
- Calls may be underused if there is no live issue or preparation workflow.
- Risk of implying introductions or access if that is not part of the offer.
- Rigid call frequency may mismatch episodic needs.

> "Most people's needs in this area are around a specific campaign… a specific election… a government announcement… a government inquiry."

#### Suggestion for Improvement

- Frame calls as strategic navigation or government engagement clinics, not generic office hours.
- Match advisers by portfolio, jurisdiction, issue type and organisational maturity.
- Create a pre-call workflow: issue context, target audience, desired outcome, existing relationship, draft material or decision needed.
- Create a post-call output: three recommendations, one stakeholder map update, one suggested next-step timeline and relevant template/workflow links.
- Potentially offer flexibility in the number of calls.
- Be explicit about boundaries: advice, interpretation and preparation and not guaranteed access or lobbying outcomes.

### Pricing

> "It would be very hard… to get us signed up to a new subscription that… might be seen as a non-critical kind of business thing."

> "If [from the subscription] I can secure $50,000 or $100,000 based on the engagement, that makes sense."

> "There needs to be some ongoing value to the subscription apart from the immediate information you can access."

**Price was generally seen as fair but barriers persisted:**

- Revenue-based pricing was seen as sensible as a fairness principle, but it is not enough. Fit depends on advocacy maturity, urgency, internal ownership and perceived return.
- Revenue-based pricing was seen as fair by some, but it does not resolve the deeper concern that many organisations want help at particular moments, not continuous access.
- The subscription model was a point of tension: it works for those who can imagine embedding the platform into ongoing operations but not for those who saw government relations as a campaign-specific need or who were worried about ongoing costs.
- Fixed two-call-per-month pricing may work for organisations that can embed a monthly cadence, but others wanted one call, ad hoc credits or project-based support.

**The strongest pricing proof is return on investment:** funding secured, time saved, better-targeted effort, fewer missed windows, stronger materials.

### Tiering Is Likely Weakening the Perceived Value of the Offer

- Having a two-tier offering is weakening the perceived value of Tier 2 as it frames the offer around templates and intelligence, which are easier to compare with free resources, AI tools, sector newsletters or existing peak-body material.
- Participants immediately fixated on Tier 1 components and if these were not seen as potentially useful, advisory calls and their potential value was not considered.
- Pricing comparisons across the two tiers didn't highlight the value of the calls but rather raised questions around 'how much extra' is being paid per call that they may or may not need in a particular month.

**Leading with Tier 2 as the actual product rather than a 'premium' offering would ensure that the product is viewed holistically as an advisory tool.**

## Motivators and Barriers to Uptake

| Motivators | Barriers |
|---|---|
| **Confidence and reassurance** – organisations want to know if they are approaching government correctly and those to whom the product is appealing see value of having access to 'best practice'. | **Budgetary constraints** – even modest recurring spend competes with multiple platforms and frontline priorities, requiring a strong business case. |
| **Time saving** – teams will pay if the product reduces monitoring, preparation and research effort. | **Unclear outcome** – features are not self-explanatory, buyers need to know what changes after they subscribe. |
| **Funding return** – price is easier to justify when tied to grants, budget asks or funding opportunities and general RoI. | **Assumed capability** – templates and alerts assume the user knows how to convert them into action. |
| **Strategic navigation** – the product is valuable if it helps leaders decide where to focus effort. | **Subscription fatigue** – many already subscribe to various platforms and alerts. |
| | **Public AI comparison** – templates and alerts can be generated by generic LLMs. |
| | **Episodic need** – many organisations need support around specific windows, not every month. |

> "The old… what, so what, now what"

> "For me to suggest an investment in something like this…despite it having a rational economic argument potentially, it would appear tone deaf."

> "To me is like a bunch of features and I don't understand how they fit together."

## Implications and Recommendations for Who, What and How

*The strongest route to market is a guided capability proposition, with advisory as the centre and templates/intelligence as enabling infrastructure – targeted at specific segments.*

### Feasible Market Sits at the Intersection of Importance of Advocacy/Policy Work, Internal Capability and Ability to Pay

**'Active but reactive' segment:**

- Ongoing need/involvement in policy/advocacy but 'winging it' due to limited internal capability/capacity
- Desire for 'best practice', capability building and process efficiency
- Can't afford (or unwilling to pay for) an external government agency but see value in light-touch external support

> "More often than not, we'll wing it."

### Ideal Customer Profile Characteristics

| Characteristic | What to look for | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Size and maturity** | Medium-large charity/social enterprise with professional staff, established programs, evidence base or service footprint. | Has enough operating scale to act on advice and pay for support. |
| **Policy relevance** | Funding, regulation, service models, community outcomes or rights are materially influenced by government. | Creates a real need to monitor and engage, not a nice-to-have. |
| **Current behaviour** | Has made submissions, met MPs, joined coalitions, responded to consultations or engaged departments — but often reactively. | Indicates demand already exists and the product is solving a live problem. |
| **Capability gap** | No dedicated government relations function – responsibility sits with a CEO, founder, board member, comms lead, fundraising lead or program executive. | Creates a gap between intent and execution. |
| **Risk profile** | Concerned about tone, neutrality, funding relationships, public positioning or "getting it wrong". | Advisory calls become a confidence and risk-management mechanism. |
| **AI fit** | Needs broad sector scanning rather than highly bespoke issue surveillance (which could be considered in the longer term). | Non-tailored policy intelligence can still be a useful input. |
| **Buying trigger** | Election cycle, budget cycle, new strategy, inquiry, funding change, regulatory reform, growth phase or recent missed opportunity. | Improves urgency and willingness to pay. |

### What Should the Offer Look Like?

#### 01 — Make Tier 2 the 'Hero'

**How this shows up:** Tier 2 IS the product, not a premium variation of Tier 1 – enabling a compelling narrative around the advisory value.

#### 02 — Emphasise Advisory Value

**How this shows up:** Highlighting experience, expertise and skills of the advisors, and potentially matching them to organisations based on profiles.

#### 03 — Provide Proof-Points of Value

**How this shows up:** Clear examples, stories of success, case studies showcasing value and cost-benefit trade-off.

#### 04 — Help Leaders Build Business Case

**How this shows up:** Offering leaders interested in the product prepare a business case for their boards (templates, guidance materials, advice etc.).

### Value Proposition

**Narrative:** For-purpose organisations are affected by government decisions every day, but many do not have the internal time, expertise or confidence to engage government strategically. This offering combines policy intelligence, practical workflows and expert advisory calls to help organisations understand what matters, decide what to do next and engage government with confidence.

**Build government engagement capability without building a government relations team.**

| Capability | Description |
|---|---|
| **Know what matters** | See the policy developments and government signals that may matter to your work, then use expert guidance to work out what deserves your attention |
| **Know what to do next** | Turn policy signals into practical, proportionate action |
| **Engage with confidence** | Use practical resources and expert guidance to prepare clear, credible and constructive government engagement |
| **Build capability over time** | Learn how to identify opportunities, frame issues, prepare materials and engage government strategically over time |

**We help you know what matters, what to do next, and how to do it well.**

### Market Entry Strategy

**Best early-entry targets:**

- State-based organisations and specialist service bodies with clear policy exposure but thin internal government relations capacity.
- Medium service providers that have evidence and community reach, but lack the time or confidence to translate this into government engagement.
- Issue organisations entering a more policy-active phase because of a reform, inquiry, funding change, election cycle or public debate.
- Coalitions, alliances or sector networks that could buy or pilot the offer for a cohort of members.

**Best channel partners:**

- State and national peaks that already understand the advocacy gap among members.
- Philanthropic funders supporting capacity-building, systems change or advocacy capability.
- Intermediaries such as Pro Bono Australia, community foundations, sector training providers and professional networks.
- State councils of social service and issue-based peak bodies that could host a cohort-based pilot.

Two-pronged approach of direct and indirect outreach/promotion to sectors and organisations where the need is already visible (list provided in Appendix A).

## Conclusions

*The offer has a credible market, but only if advisory is the centre of gravity and the product is designed around action, not access.*

### There Is a Real and Meaningful Need for Accessible Government Engagement Support in the For-Purpose Sector, but the Market Is Not Uniform

- The strongest fit is not "small organisations" broadly but organisations with live government-facing needs, limited internal capacity, and enough internal ownership to act.
- The offer should be positioned as guided capability-building: not outsourced lobbying and not self-service tools.
- Tier 2 is the actual product. Advisory is the mechanism that makes templates and intelligence usable, credible and differentiated.
- The strongest message is: build government engagement capability without building a government relations team.

**The clearest product direction is an advisory-led capability system:** intelligence, practical workflows and matched expert advice that help organisations do the work themselves, but not alone.
